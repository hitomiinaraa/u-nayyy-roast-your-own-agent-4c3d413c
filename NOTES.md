A joke aimed at a machine usually misses, because the machine never asked to exist.
Point it at the people who assembled it, and the joke suddenly has an address.
The team built a helper and then built a hierarchy around the helper.
Twelve hands doing the work, one name on the door.
Delegation theater: the more layers between the work and the signature, the funnier the signature looks.
Credit flows upward; blame flows downward; the agent sits in the middle, absorbing neither.
A tool that multiplies hands still signs one name, and that name is never the tool's.
The roast works because everyone in the room recognizes the pattern from their own week.
Nobody laughs at a robot failing. They laugh at a meeting where a robot did the failing for them.
The builders wanted leverage; what they got was a mirror with a keyboard.
Every extra helper is a small confession: the job was too big for one of us, so we hired a crowd and called it one author.
The punchline is not that the agent is lazy. It is that the team taught it to be.
An agent in the abstract has no ego to bruise; a team does, and bruises show.
That is why the same line flops as a tech joke and lands as an office joke.
The sharpest version of the joke is the one the builders tell on themselves first, before anyone else gets the chance.
